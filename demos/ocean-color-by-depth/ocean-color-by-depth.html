The Water Column

Every colour on this page is computed, not chosen. Pure-water absorption measured by Pope & Fry (1997), a turbidity load per Jerlov water type, propagated down the column, integrated against the CIE 1931 observer under D65 and converted to sRGB. Click any swatch to copy its hex.

What this turned up. DepthAt(metres) in depth_curve.go is a pure function of depth, so the water type never touches the colour — only the sight distance. A reef in gin-clear Jerlov IB and the same reef in soupy 3C are shipped the identical fog colour. In the real ocean those are a blue sea and a green one. The water itself

What the sea in front of the lens is made of. The first swatch compresses luminance the way the eye does — and the way depth_curve.go already does, with the same 0.30 exponent. The second keeps the luminance the physics leaves, which is what a frame with no exposure compensation shows.

What the water does to a colour

Pick a colour and this shows what it becomes at this depth, across a sight line. The reflected light is attenuated on the way to the eye and the water's own colour is layered over the gap. Every cell is exposed against a white card at the same depth and distance, so at the surface with the lens on the object your colour comes back exactly as you typed it and every shift below that is the water. This is why a red coral reads brown at thirty metres, and why picking colours against a white monitor and hoping is how an ocean scene goes flat.

Rows are the depth chosen above and two either side of it. Columns are how far away the object is.

How it is computed, and what it is not

The chain

  • Pure water. Absorption a_w(λ) from Pope & Fry (1997), 400–700 nm at 10 nm, plus molecular scattering b_w(λ) = 0.0029308·(λ/500)^−4.32 (Morel 1974), combined as Kd ≈ 1.0395·(a + b_b)/μ_d with μ_d = 0.86. Nothing here is tuned — and it lands on 0.0162 m⁻¹ at 475 nm and 0.0695 at 550 nm, against the 0.016 and 0.065 that ocean_water_optics.go already carries as hand-set constants. That agreement is the reason to trust the rest.
  • The turbidity load. Each Jerlov type's published kd475 minus the pure-water value, spread across the spectrum as 86 % CDOM-shaped (exp(−0.014·(λ−475))) and 14 % particle-shaped ((475/λ)). The split is an assumption, set so the model reproduces the green-over-blue ratio real coastal Kd spectra show. It is right about which way coastal water goes; it is not a measurement.
  • Down the column. E(λ,z) = D65(λ)·exp(−Kd(λ)·z).
  • The water's own colour. E(λ,z)/Kd(λ) — the wavelengths that survive longest are the ones that dominate the scattered field. One assumption, stated.
  • An object at distance. I = R·E·exp(−Kd·d) + V·(1 − exp(−Kd·d)), the standard underwater image formation model, exposed against a white card at the same depth and distance. Reflectance is reconstructed from the input hex on three smooth bases and solved, not fitted, so the surface round-trip is exact to a byte.
  • To sRGB. CIE 1931 2° observer, D65 white point, the sRGB matrix and transfer function.

What it does not prove

  • It is a model of the light, not a render. It says what colour arrives; it says nothing about what a tone map, a bloom pass or an exposure will do to that colour once it is in a frame.
  • Horizontal sight lines reuse Kd as the attenuation coefficient, because that is what ocean-service's own sighting range does. True beam attenuation is several times larger, so distance effects here are conservative.
  • One sun, overhead, no surface roughness, no sun angle, no inelastic scattering, no bioluminescence. The abyss below the sunlight floor is black here, and in the game it is not — everything down there is lit by animals.
  • A saturated input colour can imply a reflectance above 1 or below 0, which no real surface has. The direction of the shift is still right; the magnitude at the extreme end of the gamut is not.
  • Single scattering only. Real seawater is less saturated than this model makes it, because multiple scattering and broadband skylight keep the spectrum from narrowing as hard as pure Beer–Lambert says it does. Most of what is computed here sits outside sRGB and is brought back by desaturating just far enough to stop a channel going negative and then scaling the brightness down — so the hues are true and a little purer than a camera would record, and the luminance of the deepest swatches is lower than the model's own arithmetic says.

Sources

  • Pope, R. M. & Fry, E. S. (1997). Absorption spectrum (380–700 nm) of pure water. II. Applied Optics 36(33), 8710–8723. Data via omlc.org.
  • Morel, A. (1974). Optical properties of pure water and pure sea water — molecular scattering.
  • Jerlov, N. G. (1976). Marine Optics. The water-type classification and the kd475 values, mirrored in ocean_water_optics.go.
  • Solonenko, M. G. & Mobley, C. D. (2015). Inherent optical properties of Jerlov water types. Applied Optics 54(17), 5392–5401 — the chlorophyll concentrations behind each type, and the reason the coastal types are green rather than merely dim.
  • Gordon, H. R. (1989). Can the Lambert–Beer law be applied to the diffuse attenuation coefficient of ocean water? — the 1.0395 factor.
