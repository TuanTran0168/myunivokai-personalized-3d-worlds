The two and a half seconds between worlds

Changing world tears down the WebGL context and rebuilds it. That cost is measured, not estimated, and it is not going away this week — so the transition's job is not to hide the gap but to hold it deliberately. Below: what ships today, three candidates, and a loader for each world. Pick the target world, then play any stage.

A loader that belongs to its world

Not a spinner. A spinner says "something is happening somewhere"; these say which world is being built, in that world's own vocabulary, on its own palette. Each is drawn on canvas, runs on the compositor's clock, and costs nothing the main thread can steal.

What shipped

Option 3 below was approved and built. It lives in apps/myunivokai-web/src/features/transitions/, and the numbers in the timeline are the constants in worldChangeStages.ts. What changed between this bench and the shipped version is the one thing a bench cannot show: the ordering. Both genie halves are canvas requestAnimationFrame loops, so they freeze during the compile block just as the old swipe did. The destination is therefore not mounted until the departure has finished, and only the hold — DOM and CSS, transform and opacity only, so it runs on the compositor — overlaps the blocked main thread.

  1. Genie out, loader holds, genie in. The departure completes on its own terms before the wait begins, so the gap reads as the new world being built rather than as the app freezing mid-gesture.
  2. The loader is the arriving world's, not the departing one's. It is the first thing that says where you are going, and it appears the instant the old world has left.
  3. Both halves sit on the canvas layer, never above the chrome. The rail and header stay put throughout — that is what makes them read as chrome.
  4. Nothing about the wait fidgets. The loader breathes at roughly one cycle every two seconds; anything faster reads as anxiety and makes 2.5 s feel like 5.

Stages are hand-drawn stand-ins for the real scenes — enough to judge motion, layering and pacing, and nothing more. The three rejected options run at 420 ms; the approved one runs at the 620 ms it shipped with. Curve throughout is cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1), the one iOS uses for a navigation push.

What this bench cannot show: it never compiles a shader, so its “stall” is a timer rather than a blocked main thread. Every stage here would keep animating through a real one; only the shipped hold does, and only because it is CSS. That is why the ordering above is a correction to this page rather than a detail of it.
